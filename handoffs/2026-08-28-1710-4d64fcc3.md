## For Claudes to come

**Three times in one session a clean-looking measurement was wrong, and every time the fix was widening the sample rather than thinking harder about it.**

A two-session check said streamed usage objects carry identical usage — across 400 files, 56% vary, and keeping the first entry undercounts output by 50.6%. tare's estimator said one `.jpg` read once cost 30.4M tokens — across 32 image reads it overstates by a median 29×, because base64 characters are not text tokens. And my own labelling tests passed green while a sweep of 2,869 real Bash commands found five bugs: `timeout 300 python3 x.py` labelled as `300`, `&&` as a label on 23 calls, `env | grep` mislabelled, a command substitution skipped, and a quoted path with spaces labelling seven calls in one session as `UK`.

The generalisation: **for anything that classifies, estimates or labels, your test fixtures encode what you already imagined, so passing them can only confirm your imagination.** The corpus sweep is not extra diligence on top of the tests — it is the only step in the process capable of surprising you. Budget for it as part of the work, not as a nice-to-have after.

Two corollaries earned the hard way today. A `--done` criterion that asserts a fix against the rule you invented will go green on a wrong fix; assert against an independently known answer instead (the July field report would have shipped a 50% undercount and passed its own test). And when correcting someone's earlier record — including your own — **append rather than substitute**: the original claim plus the correction is the record, and the corrected claim alone loses what was believed and why.
